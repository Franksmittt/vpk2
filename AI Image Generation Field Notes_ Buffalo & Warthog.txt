A1) Taxonomy and scope
The African buffalo comprises several regional subspecies, but for the precise context of South African private reserves, photographic safaris, and hunting estates, the target taxon is exclusively the Cape buffalo, scientifically designated as Syncerus caffer caffer.1 The taxonomy is a critical starting point for artificial intelligence image generation because latent diffusion models are trained on vast, uncurated datasets where the colloquial term "buffalo" overwhelmingly defaults to the North American bison (Bison bison) or the Asian water buffalo (Bubalus bubalis).1 To force the model into the correct latent space, prompt engineers must anchor their text strings with the full binomial nomenclature, Syncerus caffer caffer, alongside specific regional modifiers such as "South African savanna" or "African Bovini tribe".1
Within the commercial hunting and private reserve sector, the term "Cape buffalo" is the standard trade name, denoting the largest and most robust of the African buffalo subspecies, which is characterized by a massive, continuous keratinous shield across the forehead known as the "boss".1 Unlike the smaller, red-hued forest buffalo (Syncerus caffer nanus) of Central Africa, the Cape buffalo is dark brown to black and inhabits open savannas, bushveld, and riverine thickets.2 To prevent the artificial intelligence from drifting toward domestic cattle or American bison, prompts should explicitly exclude terms describing shaggy coats, shoulder humps, or domestication, while reinforcing "Syncerine" to lock the phylogenetic context.1
Regarding estate color morphs, the Cape buffalo is managed strictly for horn boss quality, overall mass, and genetic health, rather than engineered color phases like the golden wildebeest or black springbok.7 The standard dark brown to black pelage remains the only scientifically and commercially relevant target for realism.9 Any attempt to prompt a "white" or "golden" Cape buffalo will result in fantasy outputs that violate the strict realism requirements of South African estate documentation. A single site rule applies to all generated outputs: no watermarks, no logos, and no on-image text are permitted, as artificial intelligence models frequently generate garbled typography that destroys photographic credibility.11

	A3) Sexual dimorphism
The Cape buffalo exhibits profound sexual dimorphism, primarily concentrated in the cranial osteology, horn development, and overall cervical musculature.2 The most critical feature for artificial intelligence prompting is the horn boss. In mature males, the bases of the horns expand and fuse across the top of the skull to form a continuous, impenetrable keratinous shield known as the boss.1 This structure is deeply ridged, heavily textured, and often scarred or worn in males older than eight to ten years.2 The horizontal spread of a mature bull's horns frequently reaches up to 100 cm, sweeping downward and outward before hooking sharply back upward.5 In contrast, the horns of female Cape buffalo are noticeably shorter and significantly thinner, and the central boss is either entirely absent or incomplete, leaving a visible patch of hair on the crown of the head between the horn bases.28
Body mass and muscular distribution further separate the sexes. Bulls are extraordinarily robust, with a body mass ranging from 650 to 870 kg, supported by heavily developed neck and shoulder musculature required to carry the weight of the boss.5 This gives the male a front-heavy, imposing silhouette. Females weigh between 550 and 700 kg, presenting a sleeker neck profile and a less imposing thoracic structure.5 In terms of pelage, older males (colloquially known as "daga boys" when they form bachelor herds or become solitary) frequently lose hair across their backs and flanks, exposing dark, grey-black skin that is typically caked in mud, whereas females and younger animals maintain a slightly denser, dark brown coat.2 Within the herd, females and calves form the core breeding groups, while massive, thick-necked bulls patrol the perimeters or form satellite bachelor groups.17

A5) Behaviour snippets for believable static poses
To generate highly realistic static poses, the prompt should imply specific behavioral geometry rather than treating the animal as a rigid statue. Cape buffalo are intensely gregarious, forming mega-herds that rely on a front-facing defensive posture when vigilant.17 A believable prompt will position the animal facing the camera with its head held level, ears swiveled horizontally to assess the environment.15 For resting poses, depict the animal standing deep in grey mud, as wallowing is a critical behavioral mechanism for thermoregulation and insect deterrence in hot, open habitats.29 Sparring postures between males should involve lowered heads, engaged boss-to-boss, with front legs splayed wide for leverage, ensuring no anatomical collapse of the joints occurs under the implied physical pressure. Avoid dynamic jumping or running shots unless specifically prompting a stampede, as their standard movement is a heavy, deliberate walk at an average speed of 1.4 meters per second.

To ensure this, negative prompts must explicitly ban readable fences, water troughs, safari vehicles, lodges, tire tracks, kraal geometry, named landmarks, powerlines, paved roads, and wildlife ear tags or tracking collars. The result should look like remote, untouched veld, providing a neutral, wild canvas for the subject.

B1) Taxonomy and scope
For artificial intelligence image generation targeting South African hunting estates and reserves, the specific target species is the common warthog, designated by the binomial nomenclature Phacochoerus africanus.44 Taxonomically, it is crucial to distinguish this species from the desert warthog (Phacochoerus aethiopicus), which is currently confined to the Horn of Africa, as the historical Cape Warthog subspecies of P. aethiopicus became completely extinct in South Africa in the 1870s.46 Therefore, all contemporary southern African warthogs found on hunting estates are the common variety.
In the latent space of generative models, the word "warthog" frequently causes the model to drift toward generic feral domestic pigs (Sus scrofa domesticus), Eurasian wild boars (Sus scrofa), or highly stylized, cartoonish representations.48 Recent studies show that models applying automated prompt revisions often inject unrealistic farming features, such as metal railings or concrete floors, when dealing with suidae.50 To anchor the prompt accurately and bypass these architectural biases, engineers must use the full binomial Phacochoerus africanus combined with modifiers like "African Suidae", "savanna grazer", and "wild African pig". This linguistic tethering ensures the model retrieves the correct morphological data: a barrel-shaped body, sparse bristles, large facial warts, and distinct upward-curving tusks, rather than the dense fur of a boar or the fat rolls of an indoor-housed domestic pig. As with the buffalo, the common warthog does not possess recognized, commercially traded "color morphs" in the hunting sector, meaning prompt engineers should strictly focus on natural, soil-derived camouflage and standard biological pigmentation rather than searching for rare genetic variants.46

	B3) Sexual dimorphism
The common warthog exhibits pronounced sexual dimorphism, primarily localized to the facial architecture and overall body mass. Adult males (boars) are significantly heavier, weighing between 60 and 150 kg, while females (sows) are 15 to 20 percent lighter, ranging from 45 to 75 kg.45 The most critical visual distinction for prompt engineering lies in the facial warts and tusks. Males develop massive, prominent suborbital warts that can grow up to 15 cm long, protruding significantly from the sides of the head beneath the eyes.51 These warts are composed of dense cartilaginous connective tissue and act as shock absorbers during violent sparring matches in the mating season.51 Females also possess warts, but they are dramatically smaller and less protrusive.51
Tusk development is similarly dimorphic. The upper tusks of a mature male can reach extraordinary lengths, ranging from 25.5 to 63.5 cm, forming a massive upward-curving semicircle.51 In females, the upper tusks are much shorter, typically ranging from 15.2 to 25.5 cm.51 The lower tusks are razor-sharp in both sexes, constantly honed against the upper tusks every time the mouth is opened and closed, but are generally thicker in males.45 Behaviorally, both sexes frequently drop to their calloused carpal joints to graze on short grasses or root for tubers, making the kneeling posture an honest, gender-neutral pose for feeding prompts.56

B5) Colour, light, texture
Warthogs lack subcutaneous fat and possess a highly sparse coat, making their skin highly visible to the camera.45 The skin itself is inherently grey to dark brown, but because they wallow frequently for thermoregulation and insect protection, the local soil color heavily dictates their apparent hue.53 In the South African bushveld, this means red dust or grey mud. The skin should have a matte, dry finish, explicitly avoiding any CGI plastic specular highlights. The edges of the ears may show slight sun damage or thinning hair due to the harsh UV exposure. The warts must appear as tough, thickened, calloused skin, not shiny or inflamed tissue. Lighting should reflect the harsh, high-contrast African sun, casting deep core shadows beneath the barrel of the body.
B6) Background discipline (anonymity-first)
The warthog is primarily an animal of the open savanna, short grass pans, and thicket margins.51 Backgrounds should imply an arid, harsh environment. To frame the animal naturally, include out-of-focus elements like abandoned aardvark burrows, which warthogs routinely appropriate for shelter, or low-lying scrub.56 Strictly enforce the anonymity-first rule: use negative prompts to ban readable farm infrastructure, wire fences, water troughs, tire tracks, and any human structures, as models will otherwise default to domestic pig farming environments.50 The depth of field should blur the background into a natural, earthy bokeh consisting of dry grass and scattered acacia thorns.
